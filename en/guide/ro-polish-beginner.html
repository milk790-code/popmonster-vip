Polishing for Beginners:
Choosing RO/GA/DA and Ordering Your Grits

Beginners should start with a DA polisher, not an RO. This guide breaks down the difference in cutting power and burn risk between RO/GA/DA, then covers grit order and compound pairing.

What's the actual difference between DA, GA, and RO?

People new to polishing often get confused by three abbreviations: DA, GA, RO. These three machine types have different motion patterns, which determine cutting power and safety. Remember one thing first: cutting power goes DA < GA < RO, and burn risk follows the same order.

  • DA (Dual Action): In Taiwan it's called a "vibration polisher," also called an "eccentric polisher"; its other name in English is random orbital. The pad is driven by the paint surface, so pressing down hard stalls its own rotation - and that stall is itself a safeguard. It has the weakest cutting power and the lowest heat and burn risk, so beginners should start here.
  • GA (Gear Action): In Taiwan it's called a "gear polisher." On top of the eccentric orbit, a gear forces the pad to keep spinning, so pressing down won't stall it. That gives it more cutting power than a DA, though it's still not as aggressive as a rotary.
  • RO (Rotary): Detailing shops in Taiwan call it a "rotary polisher"; tool suppliers also call it "direct-drive." The spindle is fixed and simply spins around one center point. It has the strongest cutting power, and the highest risk of heat buildup, burning the paint, and cutting through the clear coat. This is the machine experienced pros and body shops use.

For most car owners, a DA polisher is the right choice. It handles light to moderate swirl marks, it's safer, and it has the shortest learning curve. An RO has the strongest cutting power, but it's also the most likely to burn or cut through the paint - leave that to experienced pros and body shops.

The compound grit system

The idea of compound grit numbers comes from sandpaper: the lower the number, the coarser the grain, and the stronger the cutting power. Grit systems commonly used in the detailing industry:

  • 80 grit: Heavy cut, for orange peel, deep scratches, and paint that needs leveling. For oxidized older cars, check the paint first before deciding.
  • 600 grit: Medium cut, for moderate swirl marks and light-to-moderate scratches.
  • 1000 grit: Mirror finish restoration, the final polishing step that removes the marks left by the previous step.
  • 1200-1500 grit: Ultimate mirror finish, for ultra-fine finishing.

The MISO line condenses this system into the three most commonly used compounds: No.3 (80 grit), No.5 (600 grit), and No.10 (1000 grit), matching the heavy-cut, medium-cut, and mirror-finish stages.

5 mistakes beginners make most often

Mistake 1: Starting straight in with 80 grit

Never start your first polishing job with 80 grit. We recommend starting with 1000 grit to learn how the machine feels in your hand, then moving up to 600 grit once you're comfortable with the motion and pressure, and only trying 80 grit last.

Mistake 2: Pressing too hard

A DA polisher is designed to do the work on its own. The right amount of pressure is enough for the pad to sit evenly on the paint and for you to feel the vibration, without stopping the pad from spinning - if the pad stalls, you're pressing too hard. Keep in mind that an RO doesn't give you this warning: it keeps spinning no matter how hard you press, so pressure is entirely a matter of feel, and staying a few extra seconds in one spot can burn the paint.

Mistake 3: Using the wrong pad

  • Wool pad: pair with 80 grit, the first choice for heavy cutting
  • Coarse foam pad: pair with 600 grit, the main choice for medium cutting
  • Fine/mirror foam pad: pair with 1000 grit, for the final mirror finish

This is a starting point, not the only way to do it. In MISO's recommended pairings, No.5 can also be paired with an ultra-fine pure wool pad (for solid black and piano-black paint), and No.10 can start with a one-step mirror wool pad before finishing with a foam pad. How you pair pads and grits can be adjusted based on the result you see as you work - how the paint looks after each pass decides what you pair next.

Mistake 4: Skipping grits

The correct sequence is 80 → 600 → 1000; don't skip the middle step. For light scratches you can start at 600, and if there are no scratches at all and you just want to restore gloss, you can go straight to 1000.

Mistake 5: Not checking the temperature

The paint heats up while you polish, and above 60°C there's a risk of burning it. Touch the back of your hand to each section you polish (about the size of an A4 sheet), and if it feels hot, stop and let it cool down before continuing.

How the MISO 3-piece set works together

The three MISO compounds (No.3 80 grit, No.5 600 grit, No.10 1000 grit) make up the most common full set (we recommend having some polishing experience before using No.3 80 grit):

  • Paint condition "Light" (slight haze, swirl marks): finish directly with No.10
  • Paint condition "Medium" (moderate swirl marks, light scratches): No.5; for the ultimate mirror finish, follow with No.10
  • Paint condition "Heavy" (deep scratches, orange peel, needs leveling): No.3 → No.5 → No.10. For oxidized older cars, check first before deciding: if the oxidation hasn't eaten through the clear coat, polishing can bring it back; if it has soaked all the way through and polishing still doesn't fix it, that panel's paint is beyond saving - it's not a problem with the compound.

No.5 is the most commonly used one-step polish. For most paint in medium condition, No.5 alone can take you from correction all the way to mirror finish.

Steps you need before polishing

The full process should be:

  1. Wash: clean thoroughly with foam car wash to remove surface dust
  2. Iron fallout treatment: use iron remover to remove embedded iron fallout
  3. Clay treatment: use a clay bar with clay lubricant to remove other contaminants
  4. Degreasing: use degreaser spray to strip any leftover wax and oil from the paint
  5. Polish: start the polishing process
  6. Coating or maintenance product: finish with protection

Wrap-up

The core of polishing isn't "how good the machine is" - it's understanding the grit system, judging the paint's condition, and having patience with the process. For your first time, we recommend trying a small area in a corner of the hood first, then moving to the rest of the car once you've found your feel for it.
